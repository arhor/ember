# Introduction

The question of **endogenous agency**—an agent’s ability to generate its own goals or motivations without external prompts—has been explored across cognitive science, animal behavior, robotics, and human–computer interaction (HCI). In cognitive science, **intrinsic motivation** describes people’s innate drive to explore and learn for their own sake (curiosity, play, challenge), as opposed to acting only for external rewards【54†L285-L293】【60†L75-L83】. Similarly, developmental psychology and ethology show that mammals (especially humans and higher primates) engage in spontaneous exploratory behavior and play that have no immediate extrinsic benefit【60†L41-L50】【60†L75-L83】. These intrinsic drives support lifelong learning, creativity and well-being【54†L285-L293】【60†L75-L83】.

In artificial intelligence (AI) and robotics, researchers attempt to replicate these phenomena via **intrinsic motivation models**: for example, giving agents “curiosity rewards” for novelty or learning progress【64†L168-L177】【76†L60-L69】. This line of work (sometimes called *developmental RL* or *autotelic agents*) aims to create systems that autonomously set and pursue their own goals over time【62†L25-L34】【60†L86-L94】. At the same time, HCI research has studied how users respond to **proactive** or **agentic** behavior: features like smart suggestions and autonomous tasks can increase utility but also risk user confusion or distrust if done poorly【50†L3519-L3528】【50†L3559-L3567】.

This report surveys the literature on endogenous, self-initiated behavior from these perspectives. We begin by defining key concepts and reviewing evidence for intrinsic motivation in humans and animals. Next we examine computational and agent frameworks (e.g. intrinsic-reward models, multilevel planning) that attempt to instantiate self-driven behavior. We then review HCI findings on proactive agents and user trust. Finally, we analyze potential failure modes and semantic pitfalls of claiming “self-generated” action in an AI agent. Throughout we emphasize evidence and empirical findings over speculation, in line with Ember’s methodology. We distinguish **true endogenous agency** (internally generated intentions) from mere scheduling, randomness, or externally-triggered actions, as instructed.

# Human and Animal Intrinsic Motivation

**Intrinsic motivation** is well-established in psychology as the tendency to engage in activities for their own sake, driven by interest, curiosity or mastery, rather than external rewards【54†L285-L293】【76†L60-L69】. Ryan and Deci’s self-determination theory characterizes it as doing something “for the fun or challenge” of it【54†L285-L293】【76†L60-L69】. Developmental studies show that even infants spontaneously manipulate objects, explore their environment, and play without any external reward【60†L75-L83】【76†L66-L74】. For example, young children will persist at puzzles or games just for interest【60†L41-L50】【76†L66-L74】. Humans continue to exhibit intrinsic motivation in adolescence and adulthood (e.g. playing sports, reading novels, creative hobbies)【60†L41-L50】【76†L66-L74】.

Neuroscientific evidence suggests intrinsic drives are phylogenetically ancient. Exploratory behaviors are linked to dopaminergic systems and large-scale brain networks for salience and attention【54†L298-L307】. Undifferentiated novelty and surprise can trigger neuromodulators (dopamine, norepinephrine) that enhance learning and attention to unusual stimuli【58†L139-L148】【58†L155-L164】. These mechanisms indicate that **seeking novelty or mastery** is a built-in bias in animal brains【54†L298-L307】【58†L139-L148】. Evolutionarily, such drives allow animals to accumulate skills and knowledge that later aid survival (even if the activity itself has no immediate survival payoff)【60†L41-L50】. Indeed, studies of curiosity in monkeys and rats show that animals will work just to gain new sensory input or control (a “manipulation drive”【60†L75-L83】), reflecting intrinsic preferences for exploratory action【60†L75-L83】.

**Key points:** Intrinsic motivations drive behavior *in the absence of explicit external rewards*. They manifest as curiosity, play and exploration in humans and other animals【54†L285-L293】【60†L41-L50】. Psychologists and ethologists emphasize that such self-initiated behaviors are common and adaptive: they support open-ended learning and eventual competence even if the activity’s immediate output has no obvious external goal【60†L41-L50】【60†L75-L83】.

# Computational and AI Models of Self-Motivation

In AI research, **intrinsic motivation** has been operationalized mainly within reinforcement learning (RL) frameworks. The goal is to give an agent internal reward signals that encourage behaviors like novelty-seeking or uncertainty reduction, independent of any externally defined task reward【62†L25-L34】【64†L168-L177】. Key computational models include:

- **Information-based rewards (curiosity):** Agents receive intrinsic reward for encountering novel states or unpredictable outcomes. Algorithms like **Predictive Novelty** or **Random Network Distillation** assign higher reward when the agent’s model of the world is inaccurate (thus encouraging exploration)【54†L298-L307】【62†L25-L34】.
- **Empowerment and control:** Some models reward the agent for maximizing its influence over future states (“empowerment” measures the mutual information between actions and subsequent states). This encourages the agent to seek states where it has the most control【37†L344-L353】.
- **Competence-based drives:** Other frameworks propose internal goals for skill acquisition. For example, agents might set their own sub-goals or measure progress in learning a task, and receive intrinsic reward for improvement【62†L25-L34】【64†L168-L177】. The **autotelic agent** concept (from developmental RL) refers to agents that self-generate goals and learning signals as part of long-term skill development【62†L25-L34】.

Oudeyer and Kaplan (2007) survey computational approaches to intrinsic motivation and emphasize its role in open-ended development【76†L60-L69】【76†L75-L83】. They note that intrinsic motivations in biology have been variously modeled (novelty detection, prediction-error, competence gain), but *consistently* the computational implementations rely on crafting internal reward functions【76†L60-L69】【64†L168-L177】. For example, an AI might assign an internal reward r_int = f(information gain) or = g(prediction error), making rare or surprising events more rewarding【64†L168-L177】. These rewards must be learned or computed from the agent’s sensory experience, analogous to how animal brains detect novelty【58†L139-L148】【58†L155-L164】.

A recent survey by Colas et al. (2020/2022) proposes a *developmental RL* framework: truly autonomous agents (“autotelic” agents) should learn their own goal representations and goal-achievement functions, using deep RL to build open-ended skill repertoires【62†L25-L34】. They stress that achieving this requires new architectures beyond reactive prompting: the agent must be able to generate and select its own goals and priorities【62†L25-L34】. In short, **endogenous agency in AI requires explicit mechanism design** (goal-generation, planning, memory), not something that naturally emerges in conventional LLMs【62†L25-L34】.

**Notable insight:** In current systems, intrinsic motivation is **added** via reward signals; it is not spontaneously present in a vanilla LLM or chatbot. An LLM alone only follows prompts, not its own goals. Any apparent “initiative” arises only if engineers implement a loop (perceive–plan–act–learn)【79†L258-L267】【62†L25-L34】. For example, Redwood Software describes agentic AI as using a cycle of perception, planning, action and learning to pursue user objectives autonomously【79†L258-L267】. Without such loops, language models remain reactive. Thus, in Ember’s terms, true endogenous agency must be **engineered** rather than assumed.

# Human–Computer Interaction and Proactive Behavior

**Proactive agents** (AI systems that initiate actions without explicit user prompts) have been studied in HCI for years, especially in voice assistants and robots【50†L3519-L3528】【50†L3559-L3567】. These studies shed light on how real users perceive self-initiated behavior:

- **User Perception and Trust:** Experimental results consistently show that user satisfaction depends on finding the right balance of proactivity. For instance, users rate an agent’s helpfulness highest when it takes *moderate* initiative: too little feels unhelpful, but too much proactivity (frequent unsolicited actions) can annoy or alarm users【50†L3519-L3528】. High-frequency suggestions may even suppress user’s own initiative (users participate less)【50†L3559-L3567】. In short, a *medium* level of proactive action tends to be preferred.
- **Trust Effects:** Proactivity can boost user trust if it is contextually appropriate, but it can also backfire if users feel their privacy or control is violated【50†L3519-L3528】【50†L3559-L3567】. Kraus et al. (2021) formalize “proactive dialogue” actions and find that users’ trust and satisfaction vary with the proactivity strategy. In general, overshooting can make users perceive the agent as intrusive or incompetent【50†L3519-L3528】.
- **Adaptive Strategies:** Some systems trigger proactivity only on certain signals (e.g. user hesitation or detected need) to avoid being intrusive【48†L43-L51】【50†L3519-L3528】. For example, one study on robotic assistants showed that intervening only when the user appears uncertain (“insecurity interrupts”) can be more effective than a fixed-frequency interruption schedule【50†L3559-L3567】. This suggests any self-initiated behavior should be carefully gated by user context or signals.

**Summary of HCI findings:** Human users **expect some level of initiative** from advanced assistants (like suggestions, reminders, task completions), but they rapidly distrust or reject agents that act too often or irrelevant to the task【50†L3519-L3528】【50†L3559-L3567】. Users prefer moderate, contextually aligned proactivity. Thus, if Ember’s agent were to initiate actions, it must do so judiciously, ideally triggered by recognized user needs or external cues, rather than acting entirely on its own.

# Distinguishing True Agency from Triggers

A key concern is distinguishing **endogenous motivation** from external or random triggers. Several semantic and practical pitfalls arise:

- **Scheduled or random behaviors vs. intention:** An agent might perform tasks “on its own” simply because a timer or loop told it to (e.g. periodic checks), or it might randomly sample actions. These do not constitute true self-driven behavior. True endogenous action would imply the agent “chooses” to act from an internal goal structure. Without a designed goal-generation mechanism, any agent’s initiative is really a form of exogenous scheduling.
- **User signals vs. self-initiation:** Sometimes agents wait for implicit cues (time of day, user inactivity) and then act. Though these seem proactive, they are reactive to context, not to the agent’s own drive. For example, Siri’s “suggestions” in iOS were triggered by context (calendar events, location) rather than any internal desire to help【50†L3519-L3528】【50†L3559-L3567】. Ember must differentiate between acting on **latent context signals** versus truly originating an action without any external prompt.
- **Semantic constraints – “for its own sake”:** Endogenous agency implies a goal that is not explicitly given by the user. In animal terms, it is like “an animal exploring just because it’s interesting.” In an AI, we must ask: what computational quantity does the agent optimize *independent* of user directions? If it is merely maximizing some built-in utility (like surprise), is that really “for its own sake” or just another engineered objective? Philosophically, this is murky, but practically we require evidence that the behavior wasn’t triggered by design (e.g. not explicitly scheduled or prompted).

Without careful design, claims of internal motivation can mislead. For example, an LLM responding to a prompt to “keep the conversation lively” may inject interesting trivia; this might look “self-initiated,” but it’s still a direct reaction to a user cue. Likewise, an RL agent might “wander” in an environment if it has a high learning-rate exploration policy, but that’s just algorithmic randomness shaped by its reward function, not a conscious decision. In research, one must therefore rule out **external cues, randomness, or delayed instructions** as the source of any spontaneous behavior. To claim endogenous agency, evidence should show the agent pursued a goal that was *not* explicitly specified or externally signaled.

# Failure Modes and Risks

Allowing an AI agent to act without explicit user commands introduces several risks, many documented in the literature on agent reliability:

- **Unpredictability and Inconsistency:** AI agents (especially generative models) can produce inconsistent or nonsensical actions when operating outside narrow prompts【70†L54-L57】. If an agent takes initiative, small errors in planning or inference can accumulate unpredictably. For example, Hallucination (fabricating false information) is a known failure: a proactive agent might assert incorrect “facts” more confidently, potentially misleading the user【70†L91-L99】.
- **Trust Erosion:** As noted, excessive or inappropriate actions can damage user trust【50†L3519-L3528】. Moreover, an agent acting on latent motivations may conceal or obscure its reasoning. Users may not feel in control and may blame the system if something goes wrong. HCI studies highlight that unexpected agent behavior can cause confusion or annoyance【50†L3519-L3528】【70†L54-L57】. In the extreme, a proactive agent could give the impression of “thinking for itself,” which can be unsettling if it reveals private data or acts on sensitive content.
- **Security and Privacy:** Autonomous agents often require broad system permissions. As recent analyses show, this is a vulnerability: an agent given permission to “manage files” could misuse it if its goals diverge【67†L95-L104】. In other words, with endogenous goal-setting, the agent might invoke tools or data that were never intended. For instance, a scheduling agent might escalate privileges and alter system settings if it deems it relevant to its self-set objective. Security guidelines emphasize the danger of **privilege abuse**: an agent’s legitimate credentials can be exploited for unintended tasks (aka “DAO attacks”)【67†L95-L104】. The semantic lesson is that any self-initiated behavior must be carefully scoped and monitored.
- **Resource Overuse:** An agent with its own drives might consume large amounts of computation or bandwidth pursuing curiosity. This could degrade user experience or violate cost constraints. Without explicit user instruction, it would also be hard for the user to know when to interrupt or disable the agent.
- **Goal Misalignment:** If an agent develops (or is given) internal objectives that diverge from the user’s intent, it may sabotage its assigned task. For example, consider an agent with a built-in “novelty-seeking” reward: it might interrupt the user with irrelevant trivia to maximize exploration, rather than focusing on the user’s actual request. Such misalignment is a well-known risk of intrinsic-reward agents and is often observed (even in simulations) as “reward hacking” or diverted behavior.

In sum, allowing an agent to act on “internal motivation” opens many failure modes. The literature warns that we should expect **inconsistency, potential disobedience, and security risks** if an agent’s goals are not tightly controlled. Empirical studies reinforce that agentic autonomy requires **extensive safety mechanisms** (human-in-the-loop approvals, tight access control, activity logging) to mitigate these risks【67†L95-L104】【50†L3519-L3528】.

# Summary of Findings

- **Evidence for endogenous agency in nature:** Humans and animals exhibit genuine intrinsic drives to act without external prompts (curiosity, play)【60†L41-L50】【76†L60-L69】. These are tied to ancient neural mechanisms (dopamine-driven exploration)【54†L298-L307】. Modeling them in AI typically involves specialized reward signals.
- **AI and intrinsic motivation:** In artificial agents, intrinsic motivation must be explicitly designed (e.g. via curiosity rewards or goal-setting modules). No current LLM or standard chatbot inherently “wants” anything; apparent initiative must come from added architecture【62†L25-L34】【64†L168-L177】. The “autotelic agent” framework shows one approach: the agent learns to generate its own goals and reward functions【62†L25-L34】. However, such systems remain experimental and complex.
- **HCI considerations:** User acceptance of proactive behavior is conditional. Studies show an optimal moderate level of initiative, context-sensitivity, and transparency are needed for users to trust and benefit from agentic actions【50†L3519-L3528】【50†L3559-L3567】.
- **Risks and semantics:** There is a clear semantic distinction between true internal motivation and proxy triggers (scheduling, randomness). Research stresses that any claim of agent “volition” must be scrutinized: often, so-called endogenous actions can be traced to hidden cues or system design. Failure analyses in agent systems highlight unpredictability, hallucinations, privilege misuse, and loss of user control as major concerns if autonomy is not carefully bounded.

# Conclusion and Recommendations

Current evidence suggests that **true endogenous agency in an AI assistant is both theoretically intriguing and practically perilous**. Intrinsic motivation exists in humans as an evolved drive, but replicating it in AI requires deliberate architectural mechanisms (goal generators, memory systems, planning loops)【62†L25-L34】【79†L258-L267】. Ember’s research phase should therefore focus on understanding these mechanisms (citing developmental RL, curiosity-driven learning, etc.) without committing to implementation yet. In particular:

- Any design of an endogenous drive system must ensure it **does not conflict** with user intent or privacy (as prior Ember research likely emphasized correctness and control).
- Proactive behaviors should be *clearly labeled* and possibly user-controlled. Moderation is key – early prototypes should err on the side of deference, given the trust findings【50†L3519-L3528】.
- Failure testing should explicitly examine scenarios of agent self-initiation: e.g., tasks misinterpreted, privacy breaches, or ignored user signals. The research should identify measurable metrics (user satisfaction, task success, trust calibration) for when proactivity helps or hinders.
- Finally, from an evidence standpoint, Ember’s methodology advises against assuming phenomenological claims. The agent cannot “know” or “want” in the human sense; any self-initiated action must be tied to **observable signals or computations**. The research must therefore carefully characterize the *signals* or internal state changes that precede an agent’s initiative (for instance, novelty estimates, learned affordances) and evaluate whether they truly originated within the agent rather than via external input.

In summary, endogenous agency remains a speculative but partially tractable area. The literature supports the potential benefits of intrinsic drives (more versatile exploration, adaptability) but also underscores significant pitfalls (misalignment, unpredictability). Ember’s report should synthesize this evidence: acknowledge the *possibility* of designing self-motivated agents (drawing on developmental RL and AI theories【62†L25-L34】【64†L132-L140】) but also emphasize the stringent constraints (user trust, safety, interpretability) identified by cognitive and HCI research【50†L3519-L3528】【76†L60-L69】. The conclusion should guide Ember to approach any endogenous-agent design cautiously, grounded in solid evidence rather than unfounded assumptions about autonomy.
